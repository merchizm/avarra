# The Seven Lines Refuge

<p class="lore-lead">The Seven Lines Refuge was not a laboratory that “created” the Veyrans, nor a planned model of liberation. It was seven scattered refuges, built without full trust, by marked households who had fled or been left behind by the Vein stations after a great earthquake, after centuries as registered property, together with the villagers around them.</p>

## Before: the station households

The Vein’s network of dependent labour did not only need miners and road workers. In the stations of Asteron’s inner basins no instrument could safely read the water, stone, and unstable magical fields, so certain human households were kept with the station for the task. They read water gauges, stone tremors, and changes in light by eye, and kept shift and load ledgers. Vein ledgers called them **station households**; nearby villages called them the **ringed**.

This service was not voluntary. Households were transferred, rented, and inherited together with the station; marriages needed permission, and descent was recorded like livestock. Some children were born with signs—two nested iris rings, fine lines on the hands, sensitivity to changes in light—that carried value, because these children noticed station fluctuations earlier. In the ledger a marked child was written “good measure”, an unmarked one “cheap household”. Over centuries, closed marriage, Vein water, and stone dust concentrated these signs within those households; their number never exceeded a few thousand. Nearby villagers either ignored them or kept away as if they were contagious.

## The breaking and seven scattered refuges

Around 2640 BC the great earthquake that struck Asteron’s inner basins broke the stations. Administrators fled or died; ledgers sank under water. In a single night the station households belonged to no one—and for the first time no one was writing down their names. Dwarves were blamed for the earthquake; for the households the dwarves had been masters, for nearby villagers they were “the dwarves’ people”. Yet they were the only people who knew the roads, the canals, and the water’s moods.

After this breaking, which drove communities into seven deep ravines and the safe ridges around them, no single order existed. Seven separate refuges grew around the seven ravines, each with its own water, its own households, and its own fear. In the first decades they fought over water and grain: one refuge cut a neighbour’s canal; another pulled up road stones and hid them. The ravines later filled with water; old canals sometimes flowed backward, road markers shifted, and harvest calendars stopped agreeing. When winter and raiders became a common danger, the refuges built open routes joining water gates, grain stores, road stones, refuge courts, and watch houses. This shared system was later called the **Seven Lines**.

The Lines were not a wall. Every adult could be responsible not to a household but to water, bread, care, road, children, record, or watch. Meridian schools later wrote an orderly story tying each of seven lines to one of the seven duties; in reality every Line did all of them. The first records were not perfect bureaucracy; they were an effort not to forget who was missing or in need.

## The Guest Ledger

By the end of the third winter hungry villagers began arriving at the refuges. The Lines accepted them, but wrote them in a separate ledger as **guests**. Guests were served last in rations and first on watch. Those who had once been unwritten were now writing others down.

This ledger divided the Lines. Some guests dispersed. Some station households feared the ledger itself: for them being written in a ledger meant being owned. Households that left the Lines for the countryside are the ancestors of later **Anti-Order** movements. Those who stayed learned the opposite lesson: being on the record means not being lost.

## The emergence of the Veyrans

The signs were there before; the Seven Lines did not create them, they left them ownerless. For the first time in the Lines nobody had to hide these signs or sell them to owners. When marriage no longer required permission, the old station households mixed with surrounding families. Mixed families multiplied, the signs spread to a wider circle and became visible. After the 2500s BC, neighbouring communities began to call these people **Veyran**.

Veyran identity is an inherited peoplehood, not an ordeal, illness, or military training gained later in life. In mixed families the signs may appear differently, and no sign alone determines culture or citizenship. Whether a Veyran is Ordan, border-born, migrant, rural, clerical, or anti-order comes from lived ties, not their body.

The old valuation did not vanish with freedom. Descendants of lines written “cheap household” in the ledger were again held in low regard in later Veyran communities because their signs were faint or invisible. They are the people now called the **Unlined**.

## The refuge’s inheritance

When the Seven Lines dispersed, no single state remained. Mill villages, market towns, road families, and water councils spread in different directions. Four principles endured, each born from the reverse of station life:

- **Witness:** To prevent a person vanishing, at least two people should know their name, neighbour, and need. People whose names nobody had written had learned to know each other’s names by heart.
- **Measure:** A scarce resource must be made visible before it can be shared fairly. Measure was no longer the person; it was what made things visible to everyone.
- **Rotation:** Watch and duty belong to rotating responsibility, not a permanent class. In the stations the shifts had always fallen on the same households.
- **Correction:** An incorrect record is common work, not a person’s failing. A misread gauge had once been the household’s crime.

Later Veyran cities turned these principles into schools, water law, examinations, and archive custom. Ordan especially took measure and record into the language of conquest, often pushing witness, rotation, and correction aside. The Anti-Order movements read this as follows: what was once done to them is now being done to others.

## Different accounts

Everyone reads the Seven Lines as something different. The Meridian palace record treats them as the first model of a chosen people meant to bring order; in that reading Veyran government looks natural and superior, and the station households are never mentioned. The border Veyran account makes them the story of people who did not abandon one another in disaster, and concludes that no ancestry has a right to rule.

The anti-order movements tell it as a network that households who feared being written down fled, then returned to find ledgers; the empire reversed the refuge’s purpose. The Asteron academies tie the matter to the long environmental and magical effect of Vein water, and insist that bodily difference is not social destiny. The dwarven station ledgers say something else again: the Lines were an arrangement of registered households working for care and shelter, and those households owe the Vein. Part of the ledgers is said to lie in dwarven archives; none is openly accessible.

None of these accounts conceals a secret “true origin”; each reads the same public historical traces through a different political meaning. The real difference is which trace each account leaves out.
