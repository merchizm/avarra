<div class="lore-kicker">Era V · 1500–350 BC</div>

# Age of Crowns

<p class="lore-lead">This was not the age of more monarchs, but of temporary rule becoming durable institution: refuges became cities, cities became kingdoms, and roads became recognized jurisdictions.</p>

Population rose again after the Breakage, but the old empires did not return. Human great states were fragmented, Vein roads could no longer bind the world alone, Fey coalitions had dissolved, and even ownership of underground tunnels was contested. A “crown” could now be a river basin, forest, hearth assembly, road confederation, or monastery network as much as a king or queen. The period headings are not global stages; different regions underwent different processes at the same time.

## Crowns from Ash · 1500–1370 BC

Hundreds of abandoned forts, fields, and towns were resettled. Old imperial centres still held their titles, but could not gather rural tax or command noble armies. Nareth replaced the imperial idea with hundreds of counties, baronies, free cities, and little kingdoms. Halvek grew rich on grain stores and old-road crossings; Ardel gathered western forts, Seressa the coastal cities, Vaun northern horse-breeders, and Kastar mercenary families descended from imperial armies. The Five Crowns still contained hundreds of noble houses, free cities, temple lands, and autonomous peoples.

Veyran families dispersed from the Seven Lines into mill towns, water councils, road stations, and school cities rather than one state. Their record methods resembled one another closely, and their success with population, grain, and water led some human kings to trust Veyran clerks more than their own people. The first serious citizenship disputes over Warforged and Autognomes appeared: one city treated a being as a citizen, another as escaped property.

In the eastern road cities, worn down by war and famine, Kezran’s sermons of returning judgement spread in the 1300s BC. Some Vardûni communities received them as the final word of exilic justice; others rejected them for crushing debt, widows’ claims, and local judgement beneath an apocalyptic voice. The Kezrani path therefore became more than a new devotion: it was a sharp question of whose command a city recognised in famine.

## Root, Stone, and Water · 1410–1180 BC

The Thorn Crown made lasting law through a century of argument among Wood Elves, Thornbloods, Firbolgs, and Gnome settlements over land use. The forest was finally held not to be the monarch’s property. Firbolg funeral groves, Gnome under-root roads, and Thornblood military zones won distinct rights, while full Thornblood citizenship remained a major internal dispute.

Kar-Dur arose from a compromise. Dwarven hearths would not permit any king to control every mine after Damar broke apart. The king held foreign war, roads, and diplomacy; hearths held broad autonomy over mines, production, and deep law. Duergar rejected the arrangement, Kor-Dum and the Black Anvil kept their systems, and old Damar cities refused every claimant. Repeated floods meanwhile created water councils among Humans, Halflings, Lizardfolk, Loxodon, and water peoples. From them came the River Throne: borders were watersheds, and a source-holder could not act independently of a delta. The throne belonged not to one people but to the house capable of protecting **the whole current**.

## Seasonal Oaths · 1220–900 BC

The Veil Wars proved that even Eladrin were not one political people. Winter Eladrin gathered around the Frost Court and treated protection as preventing change and keeping old gates closed. Summer Eladrin, Satyrs, and Fire Genasi founded Goldensun near Solkar; the belief that war and harvest must not sit in one hand created the Twin Crown. Autumn resistance to any one power created the seven principalities of Red Leaf, Pale Vine, Long Shadow, Last Harvest, Copper Key, Silent Fruit, and Fallen Crown. More fragmented Spring cities of Eladrin, Halflings, Gnomes, and migrants formed the Greenwater League through debt forgiveness, refuge, and a right to begin again.

Where a person had a right to be buried could become more important than birthplace; grave records entered citizenship disputes. Lethar took its modern shape as High Elf houses built common defence around Astereth, Gnome engineers developed the first great Nine Channels, and work on Aasimar star signs created the first Silent Meridian institutions. Nearby anti-magic reactions remained local: one village revered mages while another expelled them for “spoiling crops with star-fire.”

Around 900 BC, an emperor broke a Kezrani uprising and dispersed Saelluni monasteries alongside it. Official registers placed both beneath one heading of “road-breaking”; in truth, one was a Vardûni movement of hunger and judgement, the other an entirely different ascetic network. The false pairing became a model for later rulers who recorded the Vardûni family as one single threat.

## Odraka Road Oaths · 1180–830 BC

Odraka’s roads became permanent before its states. Centaur migration routes, Leonin caravan families, Giff security, Tabaxi information traders, Orc fire farmers, and Lizardfolk delta communities made **passage-right** more important than land ownership. The first Endless Road oaths allowed a Centaur herd to cross land without owning it, with Leonin caravans and Orc herds using the same route in different seasons.

Dragonborn divided between Red Saar dynasties holding volcanic forts and claiming the First Dragons gave them rule, and Gem Dragonborn monasteries around Prism, which treated dragon dominance as warning rather than sacred inheritance. The latter became the Jewel Oath. Orcs built a kingdom in the Red Lands through controlled fire and kinship; Tabaxi cities made rare goods and stories into wealth. Odraka was never one Dragonborn continent.

## Second Spread of Spice · 1030–720 BC

Qasyrra traded but did not open its interior. Its real population, cities, and politics remained unknown outside for centuries. Qezh, Szaïr, and Ossai reached distant markets unevenly: Szaïr houses dominated trade, Qezh formed small guarded quarters, and Ossai travelled chiefly in state service. Minotaur and Leonin networks carried Qasyrra goods; red and purple dyes became aristocratic status signs, and spices used for funeral or temple practice at home entered foreign food, unsettling early merchants.

Lower-caste musicians took goblet drums, reed instruments, metal finger instruments, and complex rhythmic traditions abroad. Within a century Qasyrran rhythm reached port taverns, human courts, Satyr festivals, and Tabaxi markets; artists who had never seen Qasyrra painted dancers in its clothing.

## Hollow Crowns and Crownless Seas · 1040–560 BC

Underground, some Nimrûn cities were abandoned while others learned to live apart from Damar trade. Nine cities joined common defence, communication, and misleading tunnels to found the Nine Lantern Union. Choldrith communities made sacred lineage agreements with some Drow houses; Myconid circles rejected city boundaries, defining a city as living beings in the same memory. Grimlocks and Derro spread deeper, Kobolds became vital maintenance workers, and Warforged debates drove some to flee the hearths and form small communities. Most source material was never opened to the surface.

At sea, Sea Elves guarded tidal bounds around the Coral Palaces, Tritons watched deep storm passages, Locathah developed fishing law at estuaries, and Sahuagin defended hunting grounds with blood-marked treaties. Tortle Long Shell Roads—monasteries, families, and lodging across hundreds of islands—became more reliable than states. Hadozee never regained their old numbers but returned to importance on high masts, rope, and shore works. Piracy did not disappear: kings financed some fleets, merchant cities licensed others, and states used others against ships they did not wish to attack openly.

## Five Crowns and Measure Cities · 760–430 BC

Halvek, Ardel, Seressa, Vaun, and Kastar became the Five Human Crowns, yet hundreds of tiny duchies lay between them. Some nobles swore to two kings; some towns paid tax to one king, soldiers to another house, and funeral dues to a faith family. Veyran cities began as neutral record and water-agreement centres, then measured roads, equalized trade standards, used common weights, and arbitrated borders. Ordanor was only one measure city, though its position at river routes, grain regions, and two great land roads let its records grow fastest.

In these small cities, Aškari shrines became visible again. A face’s feast day might be when the market opened, debts were delayed, funerals passed safely, or a short truce with a neighbouring city held. When a ruler tried to bind a shrine to their own official, the dispute was never only about worship: it redefined which street the face granted sanctuary to, which guild collected offering money, and which grave counted as a citizen.

Veyran councils publicly sought neutrality but some openly refused to allow any human crown to seize the other four. They feared a single empire would reunite roads, grain stores, and water agreements under one will. They funded weak kings, delayed grain records during wars, and legally recognized a duchy’s independence. Ordan historians later called this “preserving peace”; human historians used another word: **fragmentation.**

## Ash Seal and false heirs · 610–410 BC

This was not an Avarra-wide anti-magic movement, but organized hatred first became permanent institution in some human kingdoms. In Kastar, two Changelings claimed to be the same prince, a person said to be Dhampir stole the dead king’s body, and court mages declared three different “true heirs.” The civil war ended with the **Ash Seal**. In sealed regions, shapechanging, mental magic, death magic, and divination required palace permission. Imitations varied: court magic was registered in one place, mages expelled in another, and Changelings forced through daily identity checks in a third. Lethar centralized magical education further; a mage could be royal adviser, registered craftsperson, or criminal within a few hundred kilometres.

## The final centuries · 520–350 BC

The world was richer and more populous than in the Breakage, but not quieter. The Thorn Crown made forest sovereignty permanent law; Kar-Dur held its hearths while retaining Damar claims; the River Throne argued that all water from source to delta was one political body; Lethar’s star magic made it small but dangerous to attack; seasonal states had irreconcilable laws; Odraka’s roads outlived kingdoms; Qasyrra changed foreign music, spice, and dye while keeping its interior closed; and underground cities fought wars surface historians barely knew by name. The Five Crowns depended on Veyran records, Halfling credit, sea insurance, Qasyrran goods, and open foreign roads.

Around 500 BC, some human courts formally prohibited old Aškari fertility cults. People continued leaving offerings at the same sacred tree; only the name of a Vardûni saint was written above it. This was neither the defeat of all Aškari tradition nor the complete victory of the Vardûni. In many quarters, two memories learned to live in the same stone, root, and feast table.

Around Ordanor a new Veyran thought spread. The old Seven Lines teaching said:

> If something is to be shared, it must first be made visible.

Its new advocates asked:

> **If something can be measured, why should it not be governed?**

From the 370s BC, cities around Ordanor built common grain standards, road guards, and a permanent record council. Human kings saw another neutral trading union; Kar-Dur dismissed it, Lethar watched, and the Thorn Crown protested new frontier roads. No one knew these cities would build one of Avarra’s greatest empires within generations. The Age of Crowns ended with great kingdoms established, old peoples turned into new states, new peoples making their own law, and trade binding continents. From outside, the world appeared ordered again.
