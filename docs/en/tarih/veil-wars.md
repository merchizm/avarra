# The Veil Wars and the Root Closure

<p class="lore-lead">The Veil Wars were not a Fey invasion of Avarra. When the final governments of the Vein Union began treating Root Gates as roads, tax points, and military supply stations, human villages, Fey houses, and gate keepers were pulled into war over who could own a passage.</p>

## Why the war began

Vein cartographers could measure when a gate opened, but not fully why. The Union filled that gap with “public safety”: watch posts rose around anchors, passage received stamps and cargo lists, and some groves were cut for stations. Human travellers were genuinely safer in some places; local keepers and courts lost their say over the gate in return.

Free courts were not one side. Some made pacts with the Union for trade, hospitals, and refugee passage. Others saw gate taxation as betrayal of First Fey claims. Human villages divided too: gate stations brought work and protection, while farmers and guest households near roots paid the first price of war.

## Four phases

| Period | Event | Lasting result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **2700–2670 BC · Root Chancery** | The Union maps gates and establishes watches and passage taxes | Gate law first enters central records |
| **2670–2630 BC · Open Veil** | Fights spread over anchors, watch posts, and groves | Many courts lose their Avarran houses |
| **2630–2600 BC · Withdrawal of Roots** | Fey houses close gates; some commanders try to force them open | Road networks fail; refugees and hunger rise |
| **2600–2580 BC · Root Closure** | Local councils, keepers, and court envoys accept a new oath | Gates become rare, local, and mutually accountable |

## The Root Closure

The Root Closure did not erase gates. It created common law requiring a keeper for every living anchor, a reason for every crossing, and a way home for every debt. No state could open a gate alone; no court could ignore care for an Avarran anchor. The arrangement has been forgotten, reinterpreted, and abused, yet it explains why modern passages remain rare.

## Accounts that remain

- **Union ledgers** call the war scattered Fey violence and necessary security.
- **Root songs** say humans cut not trees but the right of hospitality.
- **Court stories** do not hide collaborating Fey houses; freedom and comfort had costs in both worlds.
- **Village records** remember less who was right than which well, mill, and family remained after the war.

## Play space

- An old Vein stamp proves not gate access but a village’s forgotten debt.
- A court envoy says the Root Closure is breaking; the local keeper says it is finally being followed.
- A human family requests hospitality from the Fey house its ancestors protected during the war.
