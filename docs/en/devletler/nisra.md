# Free City of Nisra

<p class="lore-lead">Nisra is an old oasis city grown around a few deep wells and a tightly managed irrigation line in the stony belt where the coast’s moisture ends. Its greenery looks vast from afar; a few hundred steps beyond its walls, the land becomes dry and unforgiving again.</p>

Human garden families form the largest part of Nisra’s population, but the city has never depended on them alone. Well keepers, communities arriving with seasonal herds, desert-route guides, and garden workers rely on one another. Nisra’s freedom comes from its refusal to turn that dependency into anyone’s permanent property.

## The well commons

The **Well Shareholders** govern Nisra. Each well is apportioned among districts, but its source belongs to no person or household. Garden land may be leased and a share of its crop inherited, but a well cannot be sold. This principle has prevented great houses from rising in the city, while producing severe quotas in dry years.

People say the city gates are open to those seeking refuge. In practice, the gate opens and closes according to the level of the wells. Nisra’s greatest shame is failing to keep the promises it makes in abundance when drought comes.

## Gardens and the outside world

**Seven Shade Gardens** is a narrow belt of date palms, pomegranates, medicinal roots, and shade trees. **Stone Table** is the outer square where seasonal route communities bargain with the city over water, animal passage, and produce. **Cool Houses** is the thick-walled district of well scribes, healers, and common stores.

Nisra relies on the coastal cities for sea trade, yet tries to limit Slave Bay goods entering its market directly. Citizens would prefer to treat Sâlvara’s lifespan trade and Umbraval’s shadow registry as matters of a distant coast. Every person who escapes to the wells unsettles that comfort.

## Old words

In Nisra, the city’s memory is kept in garden rows as much as in writing. When a well is opened, the names of those who opened it are preserved not on stone, but in the direction of the first trees planted. Human families cannot claim to have invented this custom; the oldest garden lines retain the work of route and oasis communities whose names are now unknown.

Nisra is therefore human-majority, but dislikes being called a “human homeland.” For it, citizenship means protecting water and recognising another person’s right to be buried.

## SD 83: the garden that closes

The water in one well turns saline, and the Well Shareholders plan to evacuate three districts. At the same time, a group claiming to have fled Sâlvara asks not for their years back, only for a place where they will not die outside the oasis. The city must weigh finite water against a refuge debt that appears limitless.
