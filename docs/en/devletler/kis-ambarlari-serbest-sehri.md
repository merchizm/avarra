# Free City of Winter Granaries

<p class="lore-lead">Winter Granaries does not give a king a purse of gold. It promises how many days of flour, which river will carry fodder, which foundry will cast arrowheads, and which quay will repair ships. Here, war credit is not money but a need that can be delivered.</p>

Founded in a sheltered gulf where Nareth’s eastern sea routes meet inland grain roads, Winter Granaries became independent through its vast stores and river carriage. Fields surround it, but its power comes not from land itself: in scarcity and campaign, no one knows more precisely which goods can reach which place.

The city does not hold a state’s treasury. It guarantees future delivery of grain, salt, animal feed, oil, timber, cloth, medicine, and metal parts needed to continue a war. When a ruler cannot pay, it does not invalidate their coin; depots, convoys, and contracted producers move to another buyer’s priority. A besieged army is not merely made poorer. It is gradually left unfed and unrepaired.

## Granary credit

The city’s core body is the **Provision Senate**. Before opening a material credit, it cares less about promised revenue than the delivery chain: which plain yields the harvest, which bridge carries it, and which reserve store opens after flood or theft? Merchants therefore sit with millers, waterworkers, carrier guilds, and barracks suppliers.

Campaign credit is not one note. It is a chain of separate promises by mill, saltwork, rope house, river barge, and frontier store. Its sharpest weapon is not seizure but **loss of priority**: a debtor may still receive supplies, but only after another army’s shipments.

## One city, a winter army

Winter Granaries keeps no great conquering army. Its force guards walls, river gates, stores, and convoys, and includes Hobgoblin veteran families, Human and Halfling carrier households, Dwarven foundry masters, Duergar workers, and Warforged citizens. None owns the state alone; city law protects the idea that unseen work in the supply chain also counts as citizenship.

Its hardest argument is whether the Provision Senate may contract with two enemy crowns in order to remain neutral. When feeding one army means the other goes hungry, at what point does “commercial neutrality” become war itself?

## 83 AS: closed gates

This winter, two major debtors demand the same stored shares of fodder and flour. On paper the Provision Senate says both contracts remain valid; convoy guards know the same cargo cannot be sent to two places. Dockworkers fear that choosing one side invites the other’s attack, while choosing neither may make both armies force open the stores.
