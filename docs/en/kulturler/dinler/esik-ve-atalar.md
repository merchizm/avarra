# Threshold & Ancestors

<p class="lore-lead">Threshold & Ancestors is not a central church, but a family of practices around the permeable and guarded boundary between living and dead. Death is not annihilation; neither is the dead a resource to be put to the living’s work.</p>

## A guarded boundary

The tradition treats respect for a dead person’s memory and respect for their rest as the same duty. An ancestor may be named, a grave repaired, an old promise recalled—but a spirit may not be summoned to answer debts or become a witness in the living’s quarrel. Closing words at funerals, signs kept at household thresholds, and rules of hospitality during mourning all matter for this reason.

Not everyone agrees on what the Threshold physically is. Some take dreams, shadows, and graveside events as real crossings; others call them the language of memory. Their shared rule is firmer: uncertainty never grants a right to claim the dead.

## From homes to public squares

Its clergy is often a set of occupations rather than one office: washers of the dead, grave keepers, elders, night watch, and mask-makers carry different parts of the work. In cities, Threshold custom shapes funeral access during plague, routes for the dead, inheritance records, and the rights of unclaimed bodies. Supplying water and fire for a neighbour’s funeral is a civic obligation in many settlements.

The greatest conflict concerns resurrection and contact with spirits. Returned people are not automatically treated as impure; a spirit forced to remain or summoned without consent is the problem. This distinction repeatedly brings adherents into dispute with jurists over war dead, dynastic inheritance, and criminal inquiry.

## Three ways

<a href="./maskeli-atalar">Masked Ancestors</a> invites ancestral memory into a prepared mask for a bounded rite. <a href="./perde-bekcileri">Veil Keepers</a> use dreams and shadow to guard the boundary. <a href="./sessiz-sofra">Silent Table</a> leaves a place for the dead and is the least showy, most widespread custom. A household may keep one, all, or none of them.

Its hard sentence is simple: the past is not the property of the living. Memory may be carried; a person may not be used.
