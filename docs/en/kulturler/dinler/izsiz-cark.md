# Traceless Wheel

<p class="lore-lead">Teachers of the Traceless Wheel say that the “self reborn” is a comforting story invented to explain suffering. There is no permanent soul—only consequences of deeds endlessly making new lives possible.</p>

The path grew from wandering ascetics who taught among flood-struck villages rather than before temple altars. A rich person’s thousand offerings, they argued, cannot erase the Trace of someone they left hungry. Release cannot be bought; it begins by abandoning delusion, greed, and habits that harm others.

## No self, no escape from responsibility

For the Wheel, Breath returns to nature, Name loosens in memory, and Trace continues as consequence. This does not make life meaningless. It makes action harder to evade: there is no unchanging essence that can be separated from what a person has done.

“The river is never the same river,” a common lesson says, “yet it still carves the shore.” A person does not return as the same person, but the path they cut becomes another life’s hardship or possibility.

Practices include the Empty Bowl Sitting, daily Three Repairs, a season of travel without a family name, and open lessons whose donations are publicly recorded. The Wheel rejects most dynastic-rebirth claims as power made sacred. Its greatest contradiction is wealth: ascetic communities gathered land and wayhouses, and must repeatedly turn their sharpest criticism upon themselves.

For this path, “Can an elf be born human?” is the wrong question. If no permanent people-essence exists, what changes is not kind but consequence. That makes the Wheel open to many excluded communities and threatening to inheritance law.
