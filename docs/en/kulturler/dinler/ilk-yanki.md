# First Echo

<p class="lore-lead">First Echo teaches that the world’s oldest memory speaks through stone: pressure, fracture, crystal, and resonance. Stone is not silent; it is a language too slow for human ears and strong enough to carry oaths across generations.</p>

## Name, trace, and weight

An inscribed name preserves a person’s trace in the world. A treaty, grave, or boundary stone is not just a document but a witness carrying the weight of its words. Erasing an agreement is therefore called “Silencing the Oath,” an act heavier than signing one in many First Echo communities.

Stone memory is not assumed flawless. A crack does not mean the stone lies; it means it records another pressure too. That idea is used in old law to seek the trace of a forgotten party, worker, or exile. A reader asks not only what an inscription says, but what stone bears it and through whose labour it was written.

## Listeners and makers

Deep Listeners insist that sealed structures be listened to before opening. This may be as simple as tapping a wall or as serious as keeping vibration records for days. Children of the Anvil call craft a continuation of the world’s creative power; good work knows a material’s limits. Hard Vein interpreters favour shaping stone through will, and often argue with gentler branches.

In mining settlements, First Echo has become the religious language of safety. Before a new seam is struck, workers speak their names, recall old collapses, and record the ground’s voice in the shift book. The faith asks why a warning was ignored instead of closing an accident under the name of fate.
