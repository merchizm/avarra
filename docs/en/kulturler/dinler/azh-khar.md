# Azh-Khar and the Blind Face

<p class="lore-lead">Azh-Khar is not a life-giving Sun but the power that lessens water, burns skin, makes shade precious, and takes its due. Qasyrrans hold it sacred not because they love it, but because they cannot escape it.</p>

## The Sun that takes its due

Whether Azh-Khar is good or evil is meaningless in Qasyrra theology. It exists, sees, burns, and takes its share. It is therefore not merely worshipped: **Azh-Khar is fed.** The basic offering is drinkable water, poured into black-stone vessels and allowed to evaporate in the Sun—a small share given to preserve a larger one.

Heavier rites use oil, valuable spice, animals, and blood. In some legal periods, leaving a prisoner or war captive in an unshaded place was understood not only as execution but as surrender to Azh-Khar. Voluntary offering bears greater status. Even the return of a Neshar who has endured hours without water can be a source of fear and argument.

Some temples place an open pit or black-stone court directly beneath the noon Sun. Stones changed by centuries of water, oil, spice, and blood retain their colour and scent. Bodies left to the Sun are later wrapped, preserved, and returned to family memory: after Azh-Khar has taken its due, what remains belongs to the family.

## Neshar: priesthood beyond peoplehood

Neshar is not a people but Qasyrra’s priestly order. Qezh, Szaïr, Ossai, and people of dependent communities may join. At high rank, birth is difficult to read: faces are masked, bodies covered in heavy ceremonial cloth, and solar rings, chains, seals, bones, and offering fragments displace individual identity. This is why early foreigners mistook high Neshar for a separate race.

Neshar administer funerals, offerings, solar calendars, temple economy, cosmological records, dynasty rites, and in some regions contracts and oath witnessing. Men are not excluded, but women’s historical success in long physical rites beneath the Sun made high office female-dominated. A difference born from desert conditions later acquired divine explanation.

## The Moon: Azh-Khar’s Blind Face

The Moon is not a rival god; it is Azh-Khar’s closed, **Blind Face**. In daylight the god’s gaze is on the world; at night it closes its eyes and makes a brief space in which people can breathe. Night is therefore not only coolness but social time against daytime state, caste, veil, and ritual pressure.

At the full moon, faces are uncovered, heavy veils lifted, some sacred fires extinguished, and water left in moonlight. Water that has seen the Moon but has not yet been “seen” by the Sun may be drunk before first light or used in a child’s first offering. The new moon is uneasy because the god’s face is uncertain. An eclipse is worse: when even the Blind Face vanishes, no one knows what looks down. Doors close, wells are covered, and some cities postpone offerings.

## The ethics of night

Day is heavy cloth, shade, state duty, and discipline. Night permits unveiling, table, scent, music, dance, and intimate life. Full-moon courts may hold great meals, spiced drink, poetry, masks, and erotic performance. This is not uniform: some Neshar readings are stricter, while some courts extend night’s inviolability further.

To Qasyrra this is not hypocrisy. The question is not nakedness but gaze: while Azh-Khar sees, the body is protected; when its eyes close, the body belongs to the person again.
