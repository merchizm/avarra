# Silent Current Monasteries

<p class="lore-lead">For the Silent Current, the first sign of leaving the cycle is reducing another person’s suffering. Its monasteries resemble wayhouses, hospitals, funeral homes, and open kitchens more than towers.</p>

The path grew from old ascetic traditions. Its teaching is plain: seeking one’s own release never permits ignoring a hungry neighbour. Compassion is not sentiment but a material obligation to provide water, shelter, treatment, and safe passage.

## Four vows of care

- **Open Bowl:** A traveller is fed without regard to people, faith, or coin.
- **Empty Bed:** A person displaced by war, flood, or exile receives three free nights.
- **Last Water:** Even an enemy dead is not denied water from the basin.
- **Do No Harm:** Violence for revenge is refused; every monastery argues separately about force used to protect others.

## Non-violence and guards

The difficult question is practical: who protects a hundred refugees behind a monastery door from armed raiders? Some houses bear no weapon and offer food even to attackers. Others hire escorts only for road safety. A few frontier houses have become garrisons. This is not an old contradiction resolved; it is the path’s living tension.

## Land, records, and threat

As monasteries operated mills, granaries, wayhouses, clinics, and schools, they gained vast land. Their networks can sustain thousands in famine. In peace, rulers call their exemptions lost tax. Their birth and death archives are equally valuable: when a court wants to erase a rival heir, a Silent Current archive is often the first place it seeks.

Around 2100 BC, many of the first Sairavans reaching Vardûni diaspora cities were Silent Current teachers. Some quarters embraced their hospitals; some jurists closed their lessons. Both traditions nevertheless often meet at the same table over traveller care and debt release.
