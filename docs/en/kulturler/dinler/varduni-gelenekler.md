# Vardûni Traditions

<p class="lore-lead">When Vardûn fell, its stone, wells, and palace walls remained. Its people said the defeat was not the death of a city god: God had never belonged to Vardûn; Vardûn had failed to understand God.</p>

**Vardûni** is not one religion but a family of revelations born from the collapse of a small human sacred basin. Vardûn was once a kingdom or cluster of oasis cities with local guardian faces, offerings, and dynasties. During war, exile, and the state’s dissolution, witnesses began to claim that Vardûn’s guardian was not only a city’s god but the God of all existence. This was not easy consolation. With the city burned, the people scattered, and holy ground occupied, “God was not defeated” turned political loss into theological conflict.

Vardûni traditions do not share one proper name for God. **The One Voice**, **the Uncarried Name**, **Keeper of the Word**, **Distant Judgement**, and **Source of Mercy** are titles used by different branches. Most say they point to the same God; most also know exactly where another title crosses a line. Vardûn is therefore not the god’s name, but the place of the first great dispute.

## The break before revelation

Vardûni traditions emerged amid older Aškari city cults. The earliest tablets still name well-faces, road offerings, and local guardians. Later readers call these false gods, angels, saints, tested spirits, or local powers people misunderstood. Vardûni expansion thus often advanced not by destroying a shrine, but by renaming it. A fertility face might become a saint’s threshold, a city stone a memory of creation, an old feast a pilgrimage day.

The transformation was not peaceful. Icon breaking, shrine taxation, hidden stones, and arguments over which names could be spoken created political consequences in every human city. Vardûni critique says that God cannot be closed inside an object. The Aškari reply is equally sharp: people stand before that face not to contain God, but to approach one another.

## Tablets, letters, and seals

Vardûni sacred authority cannot fit inside a single book. Every major branch argues over the weight of three kinds of source:

- **Root Tablets** are short, contradictory writings from before Vardûn’s fall. Old city language and the first claims of one God live beside one another.
- **Exile Letters** are sermons, law, and mourning texts sent by the first witnesses or their circles to scattered communities.
- **Seals** are later prayer calendars, court judgments, pilgrimage books, and commentaries.

Vardûni readers therefore ask not only what a text says, but in whose hands it survived. A tablet from an imperial archive is not necessarily clean; an oral memory never set to writing is not necessarily invented. Textual dispute is also a dispute over class, language, and access.

## Eight Prophets or Five Seals?

This is the family’s most visible division. The **Eight Witnesses** position says God gave direction eight times for different peoples and ages. The **Five Seals** position says the first three names were teachers, community leaders, or later-expanded ancestors rather than prophets.

The numerical dispute is not dry history. Those who name eight accept that the closed early communities also share in revelation. Those who name five place revelation in a more universal, orderly, and often more centralized line. None of the ways below is merely another’s lesser sect: each has made distinct religion, law, and institution.

## Closed ways and open call

The first three ways have survived by narrowing. Some branches of Root Covenant Lineages, Wandering Witnesses, and Sealed Table make belonging depend on a founding ancestor, an accepted household chain, or a particular exile descent. This is not a racial rule: a family may contain several peoples, while two people of the same people can hold different status because one line is accepted and another is not.

The great ways beginning with Raveth reject that boundary. If God does not belong to one city, revelation cannot belong only to Vardûn descent. That idea turns diaspora into networks of pilgrimage, schools, shrines, and aid. It can also be used for conquest: rulers have often blurred the difference between carrying truth and breaking another people’s law.

## The dangerous question of Aasimar

In a Vardûni world, an Aasimar saying “revelation came to me” is never merely making a personal claim. It creates the possibility of a new sect, a new pilgrimage centre, a succession dispute, and a war. Some Aasimar used the claim to gather followers, donations, or a throne. Others were left alone; others were exiled because of the sign they carried.

Lethar’s control of Aasimar children and prophetic claims has three names in Vardûni commentary: persecution, necessary caution, or proof that God’s voice belongs to no body. No reading has conclusively won.

## Vardûn in history

Vardûn’s regional fall coincided with the return of human empires in the Age of Veins. Root Tablets and the first Exile Letters remained scattered between imperial archives and fugitive family records. When routes closed in the Breakage, diaspora communities began making law without news of one another; the Eight and Five dispute sharpened there. In the Age of Crowns, pilgrimage, orders, shrine schools, and religious wars became large enough to steer human politics.

Around 2100 BC, monks of the [Sairava Faiths](./sairava-dinleri) reached the same road networks. Teachings of rebirth and Trace strengthened institutions of mercy in some Vardûni cities, while other shrines treated them as a dangerous reading that blurred the account of a single life.

## Present tensions

In a Vardûni city, the pressing question is rarely “Does God exist?” People ask who may read the text, hold pilgrimage money, break an image, protect an Aasimar child, and decide whether an old Aškari face is saint or false god. A household may join a quarter’s Aškari rite and recite Vardûni funeral prayers; official shrines may call this deviation, but city life is usually more complicated.

Vardûni inheritance is therefore unifying and divisive at once. The idea of a God open to all never settles who truly counts as “all.”
