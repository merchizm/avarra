# Chain Mirror

<p class="lore-lead">The Chain Mirror is one of Theskar’s plainest betrayals: a false mystery made by masters, presenting submission in slavery as the price of recognition and a path after death.</p>

These societies rarely say “remain enslaved.” They use a more effective lie: the obedient will be protected at Death’s Door, while anyone who flees or witnesses against a master will remain unnamed. Some promise that enslaved people may hear their dead again if they do not disturb the household’s order.

They arise around mines, plantations, galleys, and great-house economies. Masters and their priests seek to maintain labour not only with force, but through fear, burial rights, family separation, and promises about the other side. The Chain Mirror is therefore more than religious error: it is a technology of control over enslaved people.

## The mirror’s lie

At Theskar’s Black Shore, a person meets the truth of their own life. The Chain Mirror reverses this: it shows an enslaved person not their truth but the story their owner wrote about them. Name, family, dead, and future are reduced to one record of possession.

Some cells select a “lamp bearer” among enslaved people, offering a better bed, a small privilege, or a visit with family in exchange for reporting escape plans, grief talk, and rumours of revolt. These people are victims of a coerced system; blaming them makes the system disappear.

## The counter-mystery of resistance

Where Chain Mirrors exist, counter-practices arise. Enslaved communities secretly record true names, keep families separate from owners’ records, and give their lost people road-names in mourning nights. Some Theskarite grief houses promise escapees that they will not remain unnamed, undoing the owner’s language of fear.

Their secrecy exists not to collect power but to keep one another alive and remembered. Mainstream Theskar treats resistance to the Chain Mirror as more than removing false priests: it means returning names, families, and burial rights that were stolen.
