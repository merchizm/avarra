# Sairava Basin and Monastic Networks

<p class="lore-lead">Sairava is not a border line. It is a civilisation-memory linking floodplains, nine old fords, wayhouses, and networks of care reaching far beyond the river.</p>

At the basin’s heart lie waterways whose seasonal floods sustain cities and can erase them. Old temple cities grew by governing canals, embankments, and produce stores. New monasteries preserved the same infrastructure in a dispersed pattern: a clinic beside one bridge, a grain house three days away, and a wayhouse at the frontier that admits a traveller without asking their people.

## Nine fords and distant roads

The Nine Fords no longer share one creed or politics. Some are held by courts, some by independent-city assemblies, and some survive only as ruins and water-measure stones. Yet they remain knots of an old network that determines which ledgers a city treats as trustworthy.

Sairavan influence travels beyond the basin through monks, healers, and clerks. Journeys to Vardûni diaspora cities around 2100 BC are among the oldest recorded examples. No distant monastery is simply governed by the basin, though: most recognise only the same vows of care.

| Resource | What it provides | Conflict it creates |
|---|---|---|
| Water ledger | Flood and irrigation sharing | Palace claims to tax |
| Open granary | Survival in famine | Who receives grain |
| Wayhouse chain | Safety for traveller and exile | Accusations of hiding fugitives |
| Birth–death record | Proof in inheritance and citizenship | Dynastic pressure on records |
| Clinic | Care and education | Neutrality against war |

Monasteries are not a single theocratic state. One Silent Current house may shelter a Broken Circle speaker while another keeps them from travellers. This decentralisation makes the basin hard to conquer but slow to decide together in famine. A king can decree all monasteries taxable; they know the order will be enacted in a hundred different ways along the river.
