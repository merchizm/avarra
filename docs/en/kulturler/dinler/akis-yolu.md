# Flowing Way

<p class="lore-lead">The Flowing Way teaches that life must move: death is reaching the sea, while a spirit held by force is a river dammed against itself. River, rain, tide, and blood are versions of the same lesson—nothing keeps one form forever, and nothing wholly leaves the Whole.</p>

## Death is an ending, not erasure

At funerals, adherents return the body to its cycle through salt at the coast, clean water by a river, or water-darkened soil inland. The family speaks the name three times: for what was lived, for debts left behind, and for what will no longer be demanded. That last sentence matters most. The dead are not held to finish the living’s work.

Resurrection is not absolutely prohibited. A return is accepted only with the person’s clear consent and a renegotiation of obligations left behind. The Way says the returned are not automatically bound to old debts, marriages, or military oaths; this is called the Second Source. It has controversial consequences in port law, especially for families and creditors.

## Water rights and common work

The Way’s religious workers are often canal keepers, funeral guides, shore mediators, or record keepers after storms rather than temple priests. Opening irrigation gates, protecting fish passages, and leaving small stones for those lost to the river are its daily face. Among sea peoples, ancestors are not distant authorities but old currents moving through the living community.

The major argument is about dams. Some call every barrier an offense to flow; most communities condemn not the structure itself but the arrangement that turns water into one house’s permanent possession. Good engineering, in the Flowing Way, does not conquer water. It leaves water room to return.
