# Knot Houses

<p class="lore-lead">A Knot House is not a headquarters. It may have no marked door and its members wear no common dress. It becomes a house when people who look powerless hold different strands of the same knot.</p>

A dockworker knows where cargo goes; a healer knows where fever rises; an actor knows which rumour makes a court laugh; a clerk knows which date was altered; a merchant knows whose shoulders hold a debt. None is a king. Together they may see the king’s mistake before the king does.

## How a house works

A Kharûmi house receives not service but a **knot**: a concrete problem no one person can resolve—approaching bread shortage, inheritance driving two houses toward war, a lost water agreement, port plague rumour, or a false succession.

The house does not demand secrets. Each member chooses what part of their knowledge can be shared, according to their own faith and duty. This boundary matters. A network cannot compel an Aškari shrine secret, Theskarite grief testimony, or Eshrenic patient record; once it does, it is no longer a Knot House.

Often a house is visible only through outcome: a war delays, a grain store opens, lost children are found, a marriage settlement breaks. Kharûmi tradition rejects displaying success in honours or songs. A house should leave credit with those harmed, the local assembly, or the institution that will sustain the work. When it does not, cells become kingmakers or blackmail rings.

Houses have no central command. Two using the same sign may never know one another. Sparse time-codes, a trusted name, or the story of a solved knot pass between distant houses. That makes the network hard to crush in war, but lets false information, personal vendetta, and fake cells spread. Many rulers choose surveillance over prohibition: they know Kharûmis do not govern a city, but suspect they sometimes know when everyone in it will need one another better than the palace does.
